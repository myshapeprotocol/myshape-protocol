Twenty years ago, seeing a face on screen meant someone was probably there.

You didn't ask questions. You didn't check for AI-generated artifacts. You looked at the screen, and if a person appeared to be sitting in front of a camera, you assumed they were actually there. The visual signal was the proof.

Today, it isn't.

AI can generate a face in seconds. Clone a voice from a 15-second sample. Produce video that looks identical to a real person sitting in front of a real camera. The visual signal is no longer evidence of anything.

And yet every security system in the world is still asking the same question: "Who are you?"

## What Actually Broke

The break isn't in our ability to verify identity. It's deeper than that.

What broke is the **link between identity and presence**. Historically, if you could confirm someone's identity — face, voice, password, behavior — you could reasonably infer they were present. Those two things were coupled. ID check *meant* presence check.

AI decoupled them.

An AI agent can present a verified face. It can speak with a cloned voice. It can type with the same cadence. The identity signal is clean. But the presence signal — the proof that a real, embodied human is continuously there — is absent. And we never built systems to check for it.

## The Question Nobody Is Asking

Every authentication system on the internet asks:

> "Are you who you claim to be?"

Almost none asks:

> "Are you continuously there?"

The first question is about identity. The second is about **continuity**.

Identity is a snapshot. Continuity is a trajectory. And in a world where AI can generate snapshots at will, the trajectory is the only thing that can't be faked.

## What Continuity Means

Continuity isn't a new technology. It's a different question — one that humans have always implicitly relied on.

When you sit across from someone in a coffee shop, you don't re-authenticate their identity every 30 seconds. You don't check their face against a database. You just know — from the continuous stream of micro-movements, breathing patterns, and tiny adjustments — that the same person is still there.

That's continuity. The unbroken chain of physical presence across time. And we've never had to turn it into a verifiable digital property — until now.

## Why This Matters Now

Three things are converging:

1. **AI agents are becoming autonomous.** An AI agent with a wallet, API keys, and signing authority needs to prove it's the *same agent* across time — not just the same identity.

2. **Remote work and digital interaction are now the default.** We make decisions worth millions of dollars over video calls. We have no way to verify that the person on the other end hasn't been replaced mid-call.

3. **Deepfakes are moving to real-time.** It's no longer about pre-recorded videos. Live video streams can be manipulated in real time. The "seeing is believing" era is ending, and we need something to replace it.

## What We're Doing About It

We're a small research group called The Continuity Lab. We're investigating whether continuity — the property that the same entity has been here the whole time — can be made as measurable and verifiable as a cryptographic signature.

We've run 576 experiments across four independent evidence engines. We've published our specifications openly. We've built a reference implementation and a second independent one to prove the protocol doesn't depend on our code.

But we don't have all the answers. We have questions, evidence, and a growing body of open problems. If the question interests you — if you've felt that something broke and couldn't quite name it — we'd welcome your contribution.

This isn't a product. It isn't a company. It's a research question.

And we think it's one of the most important ones of the next decade.
